P|Hardening starts with a known approved baseline, removes unnecessary exposure, and verifies the result. A device is not secure merely because one product is installed. Control local services, updates, accounts, storage, application execution, and network interfaces. For mobile devices, distinguish who owns the device from what management can control. BYOD policies must account for personal data and supported management boundaries.
P|Enterprise wireless separates link protection from identity-based admission. WPA3 improves wireless security, while 802.1X supplies the per-user authentication framework used by enterprise deployments. A pre-shared key identifies possession of a shared secret, not an individual employee. For applications, validate input and use safe interfaces; transport encryption does not prevent the application from interpreting malicious input. Endpoint detection examines behavior after or during execution and complements preventive hardening.

T|Secure baseline|An approved reference configuration defines required settings and enables drift detection. Tailor it to the system's role and test changes before broad deployment.
T|Full-disk encryption|Protects stored disk contents against unauthorized offline reading. It does not stop a logged-in process from reading data that the user is authorized to access.
T|MDM|Mobile Device Management configures and manages device-level settings, compliance, enrollment, and supported remote actions. Scope depends on ownership, platform, and enrollment mode.
T|MAM|Mobile Application Management controls managed applications and their data, often separating business data from personal content. It is narrower than full device administration.

T|Sandboxing|Runs code in a constrained environment to observe behavior or limit effects. Isolation and escape risks must be considered; a quiet sandbox result does not prove safety.

P|Asset management follows acquisition, assignment, operation, movement, and retirement. You cannot reliably patch or remove what you cannot identify. An inventory records hardware, software, owners, locations, versions, and status. Enumeration discovers or lists individual components; an asset tag connects a physical item to a record. Neither proves that its data has been erased.
P|Retirement separates inventory, sanitization, destruction, and evidence. Sanitization renders data infeasible to recover according to the required method. Destruction physically prevents reuse. A certificate documents what was done; it is evidence, not the action itself. Cryptographic erase depends on effective encryption and destruction of all relevant key copies. Retention and legal requirements must be checked before deleting data, even when the hardware is obsolete.

T|Asset inventory|A maintained record of assets, versions, owners, locations, and lifecycle status. It supports vulnerability management, accountability, and retirement; unknown systems undermine those processes.
T|Asset owner|The accountable party approves business use, requirements, and risk decisions for an asset. Ownership does not necessarily mean performing daily technical administration.
T|Asset custodian|Performs day-to-day handling, maintenance, and protection according to policy and owner decisions. The custodian implements rather than independently inventing business access requirements.
T|Asset tagging|Attaches or associates a unique identifier with an asset so physical tracking and inventory records refer to the same item. A label alone does not describe every installed component.
T|Procurement review|Evaluates security, support, licensing, and lifecycle implications before acquisition. It prevents buying an unsupported or unmanageable product rather than discovering constraints after deployment.
T|License management|Tracks software entitlements and installed use to avoid unauthorized or unsupported deployment. A working installation does not itself prove the organization has a license.
T|Retention policy|Defines how long information must be kept and when disposal is allowed, considering business and applicable obligations. Legal holds can suspend normal deletion.
T|Sanitization|Applies a verified process to make stored data recovery infeasible for the required assurance level. Choose methods suitable for the media, sensitivity, and reuse plan.
T|Cryptographic erase|Renders encrypted data inaccessible by destroying its encryption keys. It depends on prior encryption and control of all usable key copies, including backups.
T|Certificate of destruction|Documents destruction of identified assets or media, including relevant custody and processing details. It is evidence of the action, not the erasure mechanism.
T|Enumeration|Identifies and lists individual assets, components, or attributes. Recording drive sizes, memory, and adapters is enumeration rather than sanitization or certification.
T|Physical destruction|Physically damages media so reuse and data recovery are prevented to the required standard. Verification and chain-of-custody records remain important.
E|Before retiring a server, a technician lists its drives and adapters: enumeration. The inventory links those components to an owner and asset identifier. Retention is checked, the chosen sanitization or destruction method is performed, evidence is retained, and accounts are revoked. These distinct steps should not be collapsed into the word 'disposal.'

P|Vulnerability management discovers weaknesses, validates findings, prioritizes risk, treats exposure, and verifies results. A scanner report is evidence to investigate. Credentialed scans see authenticated configuration details; noncredentialed scans approximate what is visible without that access. Neither is complete by itself. A missing finding may reflect coverage, credentials, signatures, reachability, or configuration, not absence of the weakness.
P|False positive means the tool reports a condition that is not actually present. False negative means the tool misses a condition that is present. A known unpatched server with a clean scan is a false negative if the vulnerability's applicability has been established. CVSS scores technical severity; business priority also depends on exposure, exploitation, asset value, and compensating safeguards. Remediation is not complete until verification shows the relevant condition has changed.

T|Credentialed scan|Uses authorized credentials to inspect configuration, software, and patch state not visible externally. Protect the scan account and verify authentication actually succeeds.
T|Noncredentialed scan|Assesses exposed services without logging into the target. It helps characterize external visibility but may miss internal configuration and installed-package details.
T|False positive|A finding asserts a vulnerability or event that is not actually present. Validate evidence before closing it; disagreement alone does not prove the tool is wrong.
T|False negative|A real applicable vulnerability or event is missed. Investigate scan coverage, credentials, signatures, and reachability rather than treating silence as proof of safety.
T|CVSS|Common Vulnerability Scoring System describes technical severity. Use it with environmental and business context; the largest score is not automatically the first business priority.
T|Risk-based prioritization|Ranks remediation using likelihood, impact, exposure, exploitation evidence, criticality, and existing controls. It converts technical findings into defensible operational priorities.
T|Compensating mitigation|Applies alternative exposure reduction when immediate remediation is impractical. Restricting access to an unpatchable service can reduce risk without claiming the flaw is fixed.
T|Rescanning|Repeats appropriate assessment after treatment to verify that the vulnerability is no longer present or exposed as intended. Installation logs alone may not establish this.
T|SAST|Static Application Security Testing examines code or artifacts without running the application to find potential weaknesses. Findings require context and validation.
T|DAST|Dynamic Application Security Testing exercises a running application from the outside to observe behavior. It can detect runtime issues without source access but has coverage limits.
T|Penetration testing|Authorized attempts to demonstrate exploitability and impact within agreed scope. It goes beyond listing potential vulnerabilities and must respect rules of engagement.
E|A scan reports an old library, but vendor evidence confirms the installed build includes a backported fix: investigate a possible false positive. A different server is verified vulnerable, yet the scan missed it because its credentials failed: false negative. Patch the exposed critical service, then rescan with working access to confirm the result. Keep severity and operational priority separate.

P|Monitoring turns events into useful evidence. Sources generate records; aggregation collects them; normalization aligns formats; correlation relates events; detection applies logic; response acts. A SIEM can correlate authentication and endpoint events. SOAR coordinates response tasks such as enrichment and ticket creation. Installing a collector does not automatically provide meaningful detection coverage.
P|Logs need reliable timestamps, identity, source, action, and result. Baselines identify expected activity but should not normalize an ongoing compromise. Tune false alerts through evidence while retaining coverage. SCAP is the Security Content Automation Protocol, a suite of specifications for exchanging and processing configuration and vulnerability information. It supports automated assessment and workflows; it is not itself a magic patch installer. NetFlow describes conversations; packet capture can include contents, subject to encryption and collection scope.
F|Generate useful events|Collect and normalize|Correlate context|Validate alerts|Respond and measure coverage
T|SIEM|Security Information and Event Management centralizes and correlates security records for detection and investigation. Good results depend on source coverage, parsing, timestamps, and detection logic.
T|SOAR|Security Orchestration, Automation, and Response coordinates tools and workflows, such as enrichment, containment approval, and ticket updates. Automation needs safeguards and error handling.
T|Log aggregation|Collects logs into a common location. Collection alone does not imply correlation, successful parsing, retention integrity, or useful alert rules.
T|Alert tuning|Adjusts detection thresholds or logic using validated outcomes to reduce noise without suppressing real threats. Blindly disabling noisy rules may hide the underlying problem.
T|File integrity monitoring|Compares important files with trusted baseline information and reports changes. Legitimate updates also change files, so investigate context before labeling a change malicious.
T|NetFlow|Summarizes network conversations with addresses, ports, timing, and volume rather than full payload. It helps identify communication patterns at scale.
T|Packet capture|Records packets at a collection point, potentially including payload. Visibility depends on placement and encryption; a capture is not automatically decrypted application content.
T|Behavior baseline|A reference of expected activity used to detect deviations. Include appropriate operating cycles and validate the reference period so abnormal activity is not accepted as normal.
T|Threat intelligence|Context about threats, indicators, techniques, and adversary activity informs prioritization and detection. Sources, age, applicability, and false matches must be evaluated.
T|Time synchronization|Aligns clocks so events can be correlated across systems. Preserve timezone and timestamp interpretation; clock skew can create misleading incident sequences.
T|SCAP|Security Content Automation Protocol standardizes machine-readable configuration and vulnerability information using interoperable specifications. It supports repeatable assessment and security automation ecosystems.
