P|Compliance evaluates whether an organization meets applicable requirements. Identify the source: law or regulation, contract, internal policy, or an adopted framework. A control may improve security without satisfying every applicable requirement; a passed audit does not guarantee freedom from attack. Determine scope, evidence, owner, reporting, and corrective action for each obligation.
P|Privacy concerns the purposes and handling of personal information, not only secrecy. Notices explain processing; consent, where applicable, records meaningful permission; rights processes handle requests under the relevant rules. Do not invent a universal deadline or assume consent is always the required basis. Data minimization, retention, access, and deletion must align with applicable obligations and legal holds. Attestation is a formal statement supported by evidence, not automatic independent verification.
F|Identify applicable obligation|Define scope and owner|Map controls to evidence|Monitor and report|Correct gaps and verify
T|Regulatory compliance|Meets applicable legal or regulatory obligations. Determine actual applicability and jurisdiction; security best practice alone does not define every legal requirement.
T|Contractual compliance|Meets obligations accepted in a contract, such as handling restrictions or service commitments. Contractual obligations can apply even when no identical statute is specified.
T|Compliance monitoring|Continuously or periodically checks whether required controls remain effective and obligations are being met. One annual review may miss changes between assessments.
T|Attestation|A formal assertion about a condition or compliance status by a responsible party. Evaluate supporting evidence, scope, and independence rather than assuming every assertion is an external audit.
T|Privacy notice|Explains how personal information is collected, used, shared, and otherwise handled. It provides transparency but is not itself proof of consent or technical enforcement.
T|Consent|Records an individual's applicable permission for defined processing where that basis is used. Requirements vary; permission for one purpose does not automatically cover all future uses.
T|Data subject rights|Processes for handling applicable individual requests concerning personal information, such as access or correction. Verify identity, scope, exceptions, and relevant obligations.
T|Compliance reporting|Communicates required status, evidence, findings, or incidents to the appropriate audience under defined obligations. Accurate scope and supporting records matter.
T|Sanctions and fines|Potential formal consequences for noncompliance imposed under applicable authority. They differ from technical recovery costs and broader reputational consequences.
T|Reputational impact|Loss of trust or confidence following perceived misconduct, failure, or exposure. It can affect relationships even when formal penalties are absent.
E|A customer contract prohibits placing its records in unapproved regions: contractual compliance. A regulator imposes record-handling duties: regulatory compliance. The company monitors configurations, reports findings, and makes supported attestations. A privacy notice explains data use, while a consent record or rights-request workflow serves a different purpose. Start by identifying the source and requested action.

P|An assessment evaluates security; an audit compares evidence with defined criteria; a penetration test attempts authorized exploitation to demonstrate impact. Internal versus external describes who conducts the work. Known, partially known, and unknown environments describe how much information the tester receives. An outside consultant can be given full knowledge, so external does not automatically mean unknown environment.
P|Reconnaissance gathers information. Passive reconnaissance uses existing outside information without probing the target systems; active reconnaissance sends queries or probes to learn about those systems. No internal access does not automatically imply passive: an internet-facing server can be actively scanned. Read the actual actions. Rules of engagement define written authorization, scope, timing, allowed techniques, limits, contacts, and stopping conditions before testing. Disclosure coordinates reporting of weaknesses through an appropriate authorized path.
F|Define purpose and criteria|Authorize scope and limits|Gather evidence|Validate findings|Report and verify remediation
T|Internal audit|An organization's own audit function evaluates evidence against criteria. Independence from the audited operation still matters even when the auditors are internal.
T|External audit|An outside party examines evidence against defined criteria. External status does not by itself specify testing depth or how much technical information is supplied.
T|Self-assessment|The responsible team evaluates its own controls against requirements. It can identify gaps but has different independence than a separate audit function.
T|Penetration testing|Authorized exploitation attempts demonstrate security impact within scope. It is distinct from simply collecting possible weaknesses or scanning without exploitation.
T|Known environment|Testers receive extensive information about the target environment. This supports focused depth and is sometimes called white-box testing.
T|Unknown environment|Testers receive little or no prior internal information and must discover the environment within scope. It describes knowledge, not whether authorization exists.
T|Partially known environment|Testers receive selected information or limited credentials. It lies between full disclosure and an unknown environment and can target specific trust relationships.
T|Rules of engagement|Defines authorized test scope, methods, timing, restrictions, contacts, and stop conditions. Discovery of another reachable system does not automatically expand authorization.
T|Coordinated disclosure|Reports a vulnerability through an appropriate channel and coordinates handling under applicable policy. Public release without considering authorization and remediation can create avoidable harm.
T|Red team exercise|An authorized adversary simulation tests defensive capabilities and response toward defined objectives. It may combine techniques across people, processes, and technology within limits.
T|Passive reconnaissance|Collects information from existing sources without directly probing the target systems. Public documents and independent information repositories can reveal architecture and personnel.
T|Active reconnaissance|Directly queries or probes target systems to discover hosts, services, or characteristics. It can occur from outside the internal network and may leave detectable records.
E|Reading a public job advertisement to learn which firewall a company uses is passive reconnaissance. Sending probes to its internet-facing firewall is active reconnaissance. Attempting an authorized exploit to demonstrate impact is penetration testing. The tester's location does not decide the classification; the action does. A scan finding still needs validation and a test still needs scope permission.

P|Awareness should help people perform safe actions in real situations, not merely recite policy. Teach recognition, an easy reporting path, independent verification, and what to do after a mistake. Use role-specific scenarios: finance handles payment changes, technicians handle vendor access and removable media, and administrators manage privileged requests. A reported mistake is useful early evidence; punitive reactions can discourage future reporting.
P|Measure behavior and response, not attendance alone. Simulation click rate needs context such as delivery, difficulty, reporting rate, and time to report. A user who clicks then promptly reports can help containment. Training should explain why the suspicious request matters, demonstrate the safe alternative, and revisit weak distinctions. Do not intentionally create ambiguous cases with no defensible answer and then call the user careless.
F|Recognize the situation|Pause the risky action|Verify independently|Report promptly|Learn without hiding mistakes
T|Phishing simulation|An authorized training exercise using controlled deceptive messages to assess and improve recognition and reporting. Design safe scenarios and explain the learning afterward.
T|Incident reporting|A clear process for promptly informing the right team about suspected events or mistakes. Users should know the channel and what evidence to preserve.
T|Role-based training|Tailors learning to a person's responsibilities and exposure. A finance clerk and field technician need different realistic decisions even when both learn core principles.
T|Social engineering awareness|Teaches recognition of manipulation such as urgency, authority, pretexts, and unusual requests. Independent verification should use a trusted channel rather than attacker-provided details.
T|Password hygiene|Encourages unique strong credentials, appropriate managers, and safe handling. Avoid sharing secrets and follow secure recovery processes; convenience does not justify password reuse.
T|Physical security awareness|Covers badges, visitors, tailgating, unattended devices, and secure handling of facilities and equipment. Staff should follow procedures without creating unsafe confrontations.
T|Remote-work awareness|Addresses trusted connectivity, device protection, privacy, travel, and reporting outside the office. Working remotely does not eliminate normal identity and data requirements.
T|Reporting culture|Encourages timely honest reporting without unnecessary blame so teams can limit damage. Accountability and coaching can coexist with a supportive reporting process.
T|Awareness metrics|Measures outcomes such as reporting rate, time to report, repeat behavior, and scenario performance. Attendance alone does not demonstrate decision-making ability.
T|Acceptable-use awareness|Teaches permitted uses, prohibited activities, and responsibilities for organizational resources. Users need practical examples and a path to ask about exceptions.
E|A technician receives an urgent request to let an unknown vendor use a badge. The safe response is to pause, verify through established vendor contacts, and follow visitor policy. If the technician already complied, prompt reporting helps contain the exposure. Training should practice both the initial decision and the recovery action, then measure whether reporting becomes faster and more accurate.
