P|Security products solve different problems along a request path. DNS filtering blocks resolution of selected domains, web filtering controls browsing destinations or categories, and a firewall controls allowed traffic. A Web Application Firewall protects web requests to an application. A Next-Generation Firewall adds application-aware capabilities beyond basic address and port filtering. A Unified Threat Management platform bundles multiple security functions; the product label alone does not guarantee every feature is enabled or effective.
P|Email authentication has three separate jobs. SPF publishes which sending systems are authorized for a domain used in mail transport. DKIM adds a signature that receivers verify using the domain's published key. DMARC evaluates alignment with the visible From domain and provides policy and reporting for failures. DMARC can pass when either SPF or DKIM passes with the required alignment; it does not require both to pass. None of these mechanisms encrypts the email body or guarantees harmless content.

T|DNS filtering|Blocks or redirects resolution of prohibited domains. It acts at name lookup and may need controls against bypass through alternate resolvers or direct addresses.
T|SPF|Sender Policy Framework publishes authorized sending systems for a mail domain. Receivers compare the sending IP against policy for the envelope sender or HELO identity; SPF alone does not authenticate every visible From address.
T|DKIM|DomainKeys Identified Mail attaches a domain-associated digital signature to selected message content and headers. Receivers use a published public key to verify it; the signature does not encrypt the message.
T|DMARC|Domain-based Message Authentication, Reporting, and Conformance checks alignment of SPF or DKIM results with the visible From domain and publishes failure-handling policy and reports. Either aligned mechanism can support a pass.
T|DLP|Data Loss Prevention detects or controls sensitive-data movement according to policy. Endpoint, email, and network deployments see different paths; encrypted or offline movement may need endpoint controls.
T|NAC|Network Access Control evaluates device or user admission and can restrict noncompliant systems. It differs from email authentication even though both use the word authentication.
T|Web filtering|Allows or blocks web access based on destinations, categories, reputation, or content according to capabilities. It controls browsing rather than proving email origin.
T|EDR|Endpoint Detection and Response records host activity and supports investigation and containment. It provides process context not available from a simple network flow alone.
T|Firewall rule|Defines permitted or denied traffic by criteria such as direction, addresses, protocol, port, application, or state. Interpret the exact implementation's rule order and defaults.
T|TLS|Transport Layer Security protects a connection with encryption and authentication when correctly configured and validated. It does not replace email domain authentication or guarantee safe application behavior.
T|NGFW|Next-Generation Firewall adds application-aware inspection and security capabilities to network enforcement. Available functions depend on configuration, licensing, and inspection visibility.
T|UTM|Unified Threat Management combines multiple security functions such as filtering, intrusion prevention, and antivirus in one platform. Bundling simplifies management but can concentrate failure and capacity dependencies.

P|Identity management separates enrollment, authentication, authorization, review, and removal. Authentication proves a claim; access models decide permissions. Role-based access follows job roles. Attribute-based access evaluates properties such as department, device health, resource label, time, or location. Discretionary access allows an owner to grant access; mandatory access follows centrally enforced labels and clearances. Identify who makes the decision and which inputs the policy uses.
P|Single sign-on reduces repeated sign-ins across services. Federation establishes trust between identity domains so one provider's assertion can be accepted by another. They often work together but describe different properties. Multi-factor authentication requires different factor categories, not simply two secrets. Privileged access needs limited scope, duration, auditing, and removal. A valid session or active token can survive a password change unless session and credential lifecycle controls handle it.

T|MFA|Multifactor Authentication combines different factor categories such as knowledge and possession. Two passwords are both knowledge factors and do not create MFA.

T|Access recertification|Periodically verifies that existing permissions still match approved business needs. Reviewers should confirm actual need, not blindly renew every grant.

P|Incident response is a coordinated process, not a reflex to wipe every suspicious machine. Preparation establishes roles, tools, contacts, evidence handling, and playbooks. Detection identifies possible events; analysis determines scope and meaning. Containment limits damage, eradication removes causes, and recovery restores validated operations. P|Evidence handling records who collected, transferred, accessed, and stored evidence. Preserve integrity and follow authorized procedures. T|Authentication logs|Record identity events such as attempts, outcomes, account names, and source information. Use them to investigate access patterns while accounting for shared addresses and legitimate mistakes.
T|Firewall logs|Record network enforcement events with traffic criteria and allow/deny outcomes. They can identify blocked flows but do not necessarily show decrypted content or endpoint execution.
T|Application logs|Record business or service behavior, requests, errors, and transaction context. Their field meanings depend on the application and its logging configuration.
T|Endpoint logs|Record local process, file, service, and device activity. They help connect a network event to the program or user context responsible on a host.
T|DNS logs|Record name-resolution requests and responses at the observing resolver or endpoint. They show queried names but do not prove successful connection or data transfer.
T|Packet capture|Provides packet-level headers and available payload at a capture point. Encryption, loss, truncation, and collection placement limit what can be concluded.
T|Flow records|Summarize conversations, endpoints, ports, timing, and byte counts. They support traffic pattern analysis but do not contain full application payload.
T|Vulnerability report|Documents assessed weaknesses and supporting detection evidence at an assessment time. A potential weakness does not prove exploitation occurred.
T|Dashboard|Summarizes underlying measurements and alerts for visibility. Investigators often need the original records to validate detail and avoid aggregation hiding context.
T|Metadata|Describes attributes such as author, creation time, size, or other properties rather than full content. It can aid timelines but may be altered or reflect copying rather than original creation.
